Is First REIT’s Divestment Strategy Creating Value for Unitholders, or Simply Replacing One Set of Risks with Another?

Does Divestment Maximise Long-Term Unitholder Value? A 7-Chapter Institutional Research Critique, Econometric Event Study & Counterfactual Capital Reallocation Model

First Real Estate Investment Trust’s (First REIT, SGX: AW9U) divestment strategy: initiated under its December 2021 “2.0 Growth Strategy” and culminating in the landmark S S$471.5 million Tranche 1 Indonesian portfolio exit completed on 20 October 2026 (with a further S 294.8 million under a Tranche 2 put option expiring 31 March 2027): is a defensively necessary balance-sheet rescue that nevertheless fails as an accretive growth strategy . Evaluated from a credit and risk-management standpoint, liquidating the Indonesian portfolio was unavoidable: the flawed 2020/2021 Master Lease Agreement (MLA) restructuring converted Indonesian hospital rents from Singapore Dollars (SGD) into depreciating Indonesian Rupiah (IDR), creating a negative carry trap that eroded Distribution Per Unit (DPU) by 16.9% between FY2021 and FY2025 and pushed aggregate leverage to a near-breach 45.7% by 30 June 2026 . By applying S S$362.7 million of Tranche 1 net proceeds to debt repayment and redeeming its S 100 million CGIF-guaranteed bonds on 19 October 2026, First REIT slashed its pro forma gearing to 15.6%, saved S$18.8 million in annual interest expense, eliminated chronic tenant arrears from PT Metropolis Propertindo Utama (MPU), and reduced exposure to Indonesia ahead of potential sovereign credit downgrades .
Core Strategic Deficit
However, evaluated from an equity unitholder and capital-allocation standpoint, the divestment plan exhibits five severe structural flaws. First, it creates an insurmountable yield-replacement cliff : First REIT is divesting assets that generated 82.5% of its FY2025 rental income at a 10.88% gross yield (~7.5% net cash yield) to redeploy capital into developed markets (Japan, Australia, and Singapore) where healthcare acquisition yields of 4.0% to 6.0% offer minimal or zero spread over local borrowing costs . Even after S S$18.8 million in annual interest savings, pro forma FY2025 recurring DPU drops 35.9% from 2.17 cents to 1.39 cents, while analyst consensus from Phillip Securities Research projects FY2027e DPU to plunge to 1.24 cents (or ~1.01 cents on a recurring basis excluding special dividends) . Second, the headline 2.1% valuation premium (S 9.7 million) is an accounting illusion: measured against original SGD purchase prices, the 14 Indonesian properties represent a permanent capital loss of S S$137.96 million (-15.3%), and the 12 post-IPO properties acquired between 2010 and 2017 lost S 210.89 million (-27.8%) of their initial SGD cost .

Third, executing the divestment 21 months after receiving PT Siloam International Hospitals Tbk’s (Siloam) January 2025 Letter of Intent (LOI) without a single replacement asset earmarked leaves the Trust in a strategic vacuum: holding a shrunken S S$0.53 billion rump portfolio where 51.7% of Assets Under Management (AUM) remains stuck in six unhedged Indonesian hospitals pending the Tranche 2 put option decision . Fourth, the pivot to Japan and Singapore has not insulated unitholders from currency or valuation decay: a ~30% depreciation of the Japanese Yen (JPY) since 2022 has wiped 22.9% off the SGD value of First REIT’s 14 Japanese nursing homes, while its three Singapore nursing homes sit on decaying land leases with only 5.5 to 10.8 years of remaining tenure . Finally, because sponsors OUE Limited and OUE Healthcare Limited (OUEH) lack a developed-market healthcare real estate pipeline: holding hospital assets primarily in China and Myanmar: First REIT has no right-of-first-refusal (ROFR) advantage in its target markets, raising the fundamental governance question of why the Manager did not pursue a full portfolio liquidation and cash return of Net Asset Value (NAV) when units trade at a steep discount near S 0.18 .

Core Question: Is divestment a strategic choice or a financial necessity?

Diagnosis: Structural Solvency Rescue

Before evaluating whether First REIT achieved an adequate price, an institutional critique must establish why First REIT wanted to sell in 2026. If the underlying Indonesian assets continued generating stable, growing cash flows, a disposal is not automatically justified simply because Indonesia is perceived as an emerging market.

The Critical Question: If First REIT does not divest, what actually happens?

If First REIT had rejected the divestment, the Trust would have breached its banking covenants in May 2026 upon the expiry of its S$262.7M syndicated debt facility. Commercial lenders would have refused an unconditional rollover without cash paydowns, triggering cross-defaults on the S$100M CGIF bond maturing on 19 October 2026. To avert liquidation, the Manager would have been forced into an emergency dilutive rights issue at S$0.12 to S$0.15 (mirroring the 2021 rights issue), permanently wiping out minority unitholders.

Verdict: Divestment was an unavoidable balance-sheet rescue, not a discretionary portfolio optimization.

Chapter 4 ·What Happens After the Sale? The Capital Reallocation Dilemma

Core Question: Can First REIT redeploy its proceeds into investments that create more value than the assets sold?

Marginal Cost of Capital vs Yield

Divestment is only half of the strategy; the second half is finding replacement investments. Exiting high-yielding Indonesian master leases (10.88% gross yield) to pay down 5.18% debt leaves an unbridgeable -S$16.46M annual net income deficit.

Use of Proceeds Potential Benefit Key Drawback & Risk
Debt Repayment Lowers aggregate gearing from 45.7% to 15.6%; saves S$18.8M/yr in interest. Creates a -S$16.46M net annual distributable income hole; cuts recurring DPU by 35.9%.
Acquire Singapore Assets Eliminates foreign exchange risk; high sovereign and regulatory stability. Extremely tight 3.8% to 4.5% yields; short decaying SLA land leases (e.g. Bukit Merah 5.5 yrs left).
Acquire Japanese Assets 4.5% to 5.0% cap rates; low-cost JPY borrowing (1.2% to 1.5%) provides positive carry. Unhedged equity exposed to JPY/SGD slide (-22.9% book value write-down since 2022); zero sponsor pipeline.
Acquire European Assets Scalable market with aging demographics in Germany. Compressed 75 bps yield spread over EUR debt; 3% RETT tax friction; distant operator insolvency risk.
Retain Cash in T-Bills Maximum liquidity; zero capital loss; positive convexity to rising interest rates. Severe cash drag: 2.8% T-bill yield vs 10.88% lost property yield strands capital.
The Bargaining Power & Overpayment Risk:

Because First REIT is under intense market pressure to rebuild its decimated DPU, prospective sellers of developed-market healthcare assets know the Trust is an eager, motivated buyer. With its equity trading at a 15% discount to NAV (0.85x P/NAV) and an implied cost of equity of 8.5% to 10.5%, First REIT cannot outbid sovereign funds or well-capitalized REITs (like Parkway Life REIT, which borrows at 1.59%). If First REIT forces acquisitions to restore DPU, it will inevitably overpay or accept sub-prime assets, diluting NAV and destroying fundamental economic value.

6.3 German Option Standalone Case Study: Is Exercise Economically Justified?

First REIT holds a proposal to acquire a ~S$520M portfolio of German care homes funded with ~S$260M equity and ~S$260M EUR debt at 3.90%. We evaluate this option across four hurdle questions:

Q1:
Does acquisition generate positive NPV at risk-adjusted discount rate? NO. Marginal cost of capital ($k_e pprox 8.8\%$) exceeds net asset yield (5.15%), resulting in negative economic spread.
Q2:
Is it DPU-accretive after debt, fees, and taxes? ILLUSORY. Delivers 0.86¢ DPU (vs 0.78¢ cash hold), but requires hiking leverage to 43.4%, leaving zero debt headroom.
Q3:
Does it improve or dilute NAV per unit? DILUTIVE. German Real Estate Transfer Tax (RETT) of 3.0% to 6.5% and transaction fees destroy ~S$15.6M of equity upfront (-0.55¢/unit NAV dilution).
